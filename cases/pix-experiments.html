Context

Pix is the backbone of how Stone and Ton clients move money. In a single month, Pix journeys alone moved R$44.3 billion across 3.3 million unique clients, at an average of nearly 24 transactions per user. At that volume, a fractional improvement in conversion, or a few seconds shaved off a flow, is worth a real fortune, and a careless change is worth a real loss.

That's the case against redesigning core banking flows from scratch. Instead, the team runs a continuous pipeline of small, disciplined experiments: hypothesize, test the smallest version that can prove or kill the idea, read the result honestly, and only then decide what ships.

How the team works

The loop is simple to describe and hard to sustain: spot a point of friction, whether it comes from product data, support tickets, or a client's own words; write one hypothesis with a single target metric; run the smallest test that can validate it, an A/B test, a fakedoor, a staged rollout, or a small POC; then read the result for what it actually says, not what you hoped it would say.

Modal Home Pix is a clean example of that last part. The hypothesis: detecting a copied Pix key and proactively suggesting the transfer would lift conversion by 2%. The result: conversion held essentially flat, a statistically insignificant -0.22% shift. But among clients who engaged with the modal, the time to complete a transfer dropped from 1 minute 42 seconds to 1 minute 23 seconds, a real improvement in experience that a conversion metric alone would have missed. The feature shipped anyway, for the right reason.

Reducing effort in the core Pix flow

Several experiments targeted the same thing: making the most common banking task on the app take less thought and fewer steps.

Nova Tela Valor moved the recipient's name and bank forward to the amount-entry screen instead of revealing it only at the final confirmation, reducing the chance of sending money to the wrong account and cutting the distance between intent and confirmation. On Stone, the change reduced abandonment with statistical significance while holding conversion steady.

Nova Tela Confirmação goes further: consolidating scheduling and recurrence settings that today spread across an 11-screen journey into a single confirmation step, cutting it to 5. It's in development, expected in Q3.

Unlocking money clients already have

A recurring source of failed payments wasn't a design problem so much as a visibility problem: clients had money available through their account credit limit, but the product only offered it as a separate, manual choice, or didn't offer it at all when a payment needed to fall back on it.

Pix com Limite da Conta let clients pay with the account limit and their balance combined, instead of choosing one or the other. Since launch, it already accounts for 30 to 35% of payment method usage on both Stone and Ton, ahead of credit card, and 7% of all account-limit revenue by August. Stone converts roughly three times better than Ton through the same flow, a clear signal to invest in Ton awareness next.

Uso automático takes the same idea further: when a scheduled Pix would otherwise fail for lack of balance, the account limit now covers the gap automatically with a single upfront authorization. That single failure mode had already cost R$145 million in lost transaction volume over twelve months across roughly 480 thousand eligible clients.

Trazer dinheiro para pagamento addresses the same root cause from the other direction, for the 15% of clients who reach a bill payment without enough balance at all: letting them pull funds from another bank on the spot to complete the payment, tested as a straightforward A/B experiment.

Built from what clients actually say

Some of the clearest signals came directly from clients, not from a dashboard.

Pagamento em lote exists because business clients said so: "I'd like to know how to pay boletos in batch, because right now the process is time-consuming, and Bradesco's system offers an easier way to do this. There's no way to make batch payments on Stone." With roughly 30% of the base expressing interest, the team estimated that converting even 20% of them would generate close to R$1.9 million a year in incremental revenue, enough to justify building it properly for mobile and web on both brands.

Upload PDF answers a smaller but constant frustration: about 20% of clients hit a failed barcode scan every month when paying a bill. Before committing engineering time to a full solution, the team shipped a fakedoor test, a real entry point with no working feature behind it yet, to measure genuine interest before building.

What this shows

In a single quarter, the same design team ran experiments spanning every stage from live in production to an early proof of concept, across a dozen initiatives touching the flows millions of clients use most. Some won clearly. One held flat on its primary metric but won on experience. One produced a result that had to be reported as inconclusive rather than dressed up as a win.

That's the point. Cofrinhos needed deep discovery because the product itself was still being defined. Banking's core flows don't need reinvention, they need a team willing to keep testing them, and honest enough to say so when a test doesn't confirm what everyone hoped.

Results

Before this wave of experiments, an internal survey asked clients what they liked most about paying with Pix on Stone. "Simple and intuitive" ranked second to last, at just 7%, trailing well behind functional reasons like no fees and instant transactions. "Has all the features I need" ranked dead last, at 4%.

Across the six experiments run since, both perceptions moved. "Simple and intuitive" nearly tripled to an average of 20 to 26% across Stone and Ton, though it still isn't the top reason clients give for liking Pix. "Has all the features I need" moved further still, from last place to an average of roughly 30%, the clearest sign that closing concrete functional gaps, the account limit, error states, batch payments, changed how complete the product feels, not just how it scores.
